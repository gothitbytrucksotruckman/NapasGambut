# Method and limits

This is the plain-language method text shown on the app's Method screen (English version). The app reads the same words from `src/i18n/`; keep the two in sync. Numbers such as thresholds, dates and the seed are filled in from `manifest.json` at runtime.

> While `manifest.data_kind` is `"mock"`, every number in the app is generated by a script (`npm run mock`), not taken from a satellite. The real NISAR pipeline will write the same files, and the app will show them without code changes.

## How the radar sees peat

NISAR carries an L-band radar. Like a bat, it shouts a pulse and listens to its own echo, so it needs no sunlight and its 24 cm waves pass through smoke and cloud.

Wet ground shines back at the radar the way a wet road shines under headlights. As peat dries, the echo fades. A falling HH line on the pulse card is that fade.

The radar sends and receives in two directions. HH listens mostly to the floor: soil and water. HV listens mostly to the furniture: leaves and branches. Fire burns the furniture, so HV drops sharply after a burn.

## The radar wetness index

For each 500 m cell, the index is the change in HH brightness since the first pass, in decibels, minus the same change in reference forest on mineral soil. Subtracting the reference removes changes that affect the whole scene, such as the radar itself or the weather.

```text
D_t     = (HH_t − HH_0)_cell − (HH_t − HH_0)_reference
sigma_t = 1.4826 × MAD(D_t over reference-forest cells)
```

## How a cell gets its condition

Each pass, the rules are checked in this order. The first one that fits wins. Sigma (σ) is the noise floor: how much the reference forest wobbles on that pass. Defaults: `change_k = 2`, `deep_k = 4`, `rain_return_mm = 50`.

1. **No data:** the pass was dropped, or less than half the cell has usable radar pixels.
2. **Fire, radar agrees:** fire was detected since the last pass, and the radar shows it too (HV fell by more than `change_k`·σ, or coherence fell under 20).
3. **Fire, radar silent:** fire was detected, but the radar shows neither sign. Low-intensity ground fires can do this.
4. **Burned:** the cell was on fire at an earlier pass.
5. **Re-wetting:** rain has returned (one interval with at least `rain_return_mm`) and the index has climbed more than `change_k`·σ above its lowest point.
6. **Deep drying:** the index is more than `deep_k`·σ below zero.
7. **Drying:** the index is more than `change_k`·σ below zero.
8. **Stable:** none of the above. The change is within the noise.

## What the radar cannot tell you

- The index is a relative wetness signal, not a water-table depth in centimetres. Field wells are still needed to turn it into depth.
- Under dense, intact swamp forest the canopy absorbs most of the signal, so the radar sees little of the peat surface. Small changes there may be missed.
- Low-intensity fires that smoulder under the surface can leave the canopy standing. The radar may stay silent; satellite fire detections still catch many of them.
- One pass every 12 days can miss a fire that starts and ends between passes.

## Two models and their limits

Shown when the bundle carries model output (data contract 0.2). Details for the data team are in `docs/models.md`.

### Land cover from radar

A gradient-boosted tree model reads radar brightness and texture in each cell: HH, HV, how HV varies over the season, and how it varies inside the cell. It sorts cells into four kinds of cover: forest, cleared or degraded land, plantation and built-up.

It learned from the 2021 maps (ESA WorldCover and GFW concessions). Canal density and drying are deliberately not inputs, so the canal and drying checks stay fair.

Each cell gets the set of covers the radar cannot rule out. On held-out 5 km blocks, the set contains the map's class about 90% of the time. That figure means agreement with the 2021 maps, not with the ground. Peat and canals still come from maps; radar cannot see peat soil.

When the model passes its checks, the land types on the map come from it, and the screen prints the share checked on held-out blocks beside the 90% target. Otherwise the land types come from the maps, and the screen says why.

Cells where the radar confidently disagrees with the older map are flagged as worth checking on the ground. Many will be mixed cells, not real change.

### Fire-risk hindcast

A small logistic model ranks cells by fire risk for the time until the next pass. It uses only what was known at the pass: the HV radar index and its change, canal density, distance to an earlier fire, interval length and land cover.

It is tested on the last two passes, which it never saw, against three simple rules, with 95% intervals from resampling 5 km blocks. It is shown only if its lower bound beats the best rule; otherwise the app shows the table and says so.

One season, one site, few fires: a hindcast, not a forecast.

## Use of AI

The land classes and the fire-risk hindcast use machine learning (scikit-learn, MAPIE conformal sets, cleanlab).

No language model runs in the app. Every sentence about a cell comes from fixed templates.

AI coding assistants helped write the software and the Indonesian text, which a native speaker reviews.

## What NISAR could do next

- A full-year record, so the dry season can be compared with the wet season that refills the peat.
- A 12-day watch through each dry season, sent to restoration teams while there is still time to close canals.
- The same method for other tropical peat: the Congo Basin and the Peruvian Amazon.
- Pairing NISAR's L-band with its S-band radar, which is more sensitive to the canopy, to separate floor and furniture more cleanly.
